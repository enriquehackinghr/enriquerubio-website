AI for Beginners Introduction
Welcome to the book

Introduction

Why this book exists, who it is for, and how to work through it without losing the thread.

This book is for the person who has heard a lot about AI, opened ChatGPT or Claude once or twice, and quietly walked away unimpressed. The output looked generic. The conversation felt like talking to a search engine that talks back. The promise everyone keeps making about the technology did not match what you saw on the screen, and you closed the tab.

That mismatch is not your fault. The tools are powerful, and the path from a blank prompt to useful work is longer than the marketing suggests. The path is also walkable, with the right map. This book is that map.

The argument runs in three layers. The first layer is reframing. AI is closer to a first hire than to a search engine: a capable, fast, generally available colleague who needs to be briefed, given context, and managed like any other team member. Chapter 1 introduces the reframe, and the rest of Part I builds on it.

The second layer is craft. There are specific moves that turn average prompts into sharp ones. Specific moves that turn one-off conversations into durable systems. Specific moves that turn AI from an executor into a thinking partner. Parts II through VI teach the craft, with one chapter per move and one move per chapter.

The third layer is system. The craft compounds when you put it inside structure. Projects that hold the library. Workflows that run on schedules. Agents that handle one task each, well. Parts VII and VIII build the system. Parts IX and X close the book with the principles that hold the whole thing together.

The structure of every chapter is the same. A short description of what the chapter teaches. A How I Do This section with three Moves you can copy. An Insights section with five observations. A To Do section with five concrete steps. A signature line. A closer that points to the next chapter. The repetition is deliberate. By Chapter 5, the structure is familiar. By Chapter 10, you are reading the moves directly without needing the structure to guide you.

This is a book to work with, alongside your real work. Each chapter has a Practice Time that ranges from ten minutes to thirty. Doing the practice is the difference between knowing the moves and using them. The book takes about a month to work through at one chapter per workday, with the practice fitting alongside your real work. By the end of the month, the library is built, the systems are running, and AI is helping you complete work that used to require more manual effort.

By the end of the book, you will have built a small working system: reusable briefs, an About-Me file, a voice file, a project or workspace, a prompt library, a few recurring workflows, a simple agent design, and a personal charter for responsible use. The system requires no technical training. It requires practice, judgment, and a different way of treating AI than the one that comes naturally.

A few notes on what this book does not do. It does not teach prompt tricks that work for a week and stop working when the tools change. The moves are durable. It does not promise that AI will replace your judgment. The judgment stays yours, by design. It does not assume any technical background. If you can use email and a web browser, you can do every move in this book.

The book uses ChatGPT and Claude as the primary tools, with occasional notes for other tools where the patterns shift. The choice is practical: these two are widely available and useful for beginners at the time of writing, transferable as tools change, and compatible with the systems Parts V through VIII build. The moves transfer to other tools with minor adjustments. AI tools change quickly. The specific buttons, names, and features inside any tool may change. The deeper moves do not: briefing, context, iteration, judgment, workflow design, and guardrails.

One last thing before Chapter 1. The book is opinionated, by design. There are dozens of ways to use AI, and any book that tries to cover all of them produces a survey instead of a practice. This book picks one durable approach and walks you through it carefully. The approach has worked across thousands of conversations and dozens of workflows. It can work for you, with the practice the chapters ask of you.

Read the next page first. Then open Chapter 1.

Before You
Paste Anything
Into AI

A small pause before the work begins. The protection it provides is large.

Before you paste anything into AI, pause. This page covers the safety habits that hold across every chapter in the book, so the practice you build from Chapter 1 forward starts from solid ground. Read it once before Chapter 1. Return to it whenever you are about to sit down with AI on something sensitive.

Do not share confidential company information, client information, employee information, student information, patient information, legal records, financial records, proprietary strategy, or anything your organization would not want placed in an external tool, unless you are explicitly allowed to do so. If you are using AI at work, check your organization's AI policy first. If you are not sure, remove names, identifying details, and sensitive numbers before you paste.

AI can sound confident and still be wrong. The output reads well even when it misses the mark. Treat the first answer as a draft. The work needs your review before it leaves your hands, and the two-minute review habit appears across many of the chapters that follow for this reason. High-stakes work requires verification. The judgment about whether the work is high-stakes lives with you.

Use AI for thinking, drafting, summarizing, organizing, and accelerating the work. The work itself remains yours. Accountability does not transfer to AI when something goes wrong, and the chapters that follow build on that assumption.

When you use connectors, understand what each connector can access before you turn it on. The connector reads, and in some cases writes, data inside the tools you authorize. Read-only access is the safer starting point, with write access added later once you trust the workflow. Parts V and VII revisit this point in more detail.

None of this is meant to slow the practice down. The safety habits become automatic within a week, and the chapters that follow assume the habits are in place. Read the next page, then open Chapter 1.

AI for Beginners How to Use This Book
Three paths into the book

How to Use
This Book

The book works front to back. It also works as a field guide when you need a specific move.

The book is built to be read front to back across about a month of practice, one chapter per workday. The order matters because each part builds on the one before it. If you start with Chapter 1 and walk through to Chapter 44, you finish with a working library, a small set of recurring systems, a personal charter for ongoing use, and a practice you can carry into any future tool. The full path is the strongest path, and most readers will get the most value from following it as written.

Some readers arrive at the book from a different angle. Three other paths are worth naming, so you can find the place where the book meets you.

If you are brand new to AI, read Parts I through III in order before doing anything else. Part I gives you the frame. Part II gets your first real practice on the page. Part III builds the library of context files that everything later in the book depends on. The first three parts are the foundation, and skipping them tends to make the later parts feel arbitrary instead of cumulative. Stay in the first three parts long enough for the moves to feel familiar.

If you already use AI in some form but the output is uneven, start with Parts IV through VI. Part IV teaches the prompting moves that turn average output into sharper output. Part V moves the prompts into durable saved context. Part VI teaches the thinking moves that change what you use AI for. The three middle parts treat the common ground between casual use and a real practice, and they often produce the biggest visible improvement for readers who have been using AI without a system.

If you want repeatable work systems, head to Parts V through VIII once you have read Parts I through IV. Part V builds projects and memory. Part VII teaches workflow design. Part VIII builds simple agents. Read the safety page on the previous spread first, then return to Part IX before you turn on connectors, automate recurring tasks, or build agents that touch real work. The system parts are powerful, and the safety habits give them the right boundaries.

The book also works as a field guide once you have read it through. The contents page, the part openers, and the To Do sections at the end of every chapter are designed for quick return visits when you need a specific move. The Insights section in each chapter is the fastest way to scan what the chapter teaches without re-reading the prose. Treat the book like a reference manual after the first read, and pick it back up whenever the work asks for a specific move you remember half of.

Whatever path you take, the practice is the point. Reading without practicing produces the same gap with AI that the introduction describes. Doing the practice, even on small real tasks, closes the gap within the first few chapters. Open Chapter 1 when you are ready.

Chapter One

AI Is Your
First Hire

The single frame that makes everything else compound.

Read time8 minutes
Practice time20 minutes
DifficultyBeginner
What you need before you start
A free ChatGPT or Claude account
A blank document (Word, Google Docs, anything)
Twenty quiet minutes

Description

I have watched this scene play out hundreds of times. Someone opens ChatGPT or Claude the way they open Google. They type a question, glance at the answer, take what they can use, and close the tab. The interaction lasts under a minute and produces a mediocre result. The person walks away thinking AI is overrated, or that it works for other people and somehow does not work for them.

I have watched this happen hundreds of times. The person doing it is usually smart and capable, and the AI they are using is usually capable too. The mismatch lives in the frame, before either of them says a word.

The frame is the whole game. AI is your first hire. Treat it that way, and everything in this book starts to compound. Treat it like a search box, and you will keep getting search-box results. This chapter is short because the idea is small. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. By the end of these few pages, you will have done one exercise that costs you twenty minutes and saves you hours every week for the rest of your career.

How I do this

Let me show this with one example. Imagine you need to draft a project proposal. The scenario is universal: a new client engagement, an internal initiative for your boss, a community project you want to pitch. The work shows up in every field.

Move 01 First, the way almost everyone does it

Here is what almost everyone does the first time they reach for AI. They open the tool and type something like this:

Prompt: the search-box version Write me a project proposal for a new training initiative.

The AI returns a generic proposal. The reader skims it, picks out a few phrases that sound useful, copies them into a document, and spends the next hour rewriting most of it. The result feels worse than what they would have written from scratch, because it has the texture of a template.

¶
Worth noting

They blame the AI. The AI is the easy thing to blame, and it is almost never the actual problem.

Move 02 Now, the way that actually works

Here is what I do. Before I open the tool, I sit for two minutes and write the brief I would give a new hire who had been asked to draft this for me. I write it as if I am talking to a capable person who has never met me, never seen my company, and does not know what I value. Then I open the tool and paste the brief.

Prompt: the first-hire version I am going to ask you to draft a project proposal. Before you do, here is the context. The proposal is for the head of operations at a mid-sized manufacturing company. The goal is to win a six-month consulting engagement. The stakeholder cares most about cost control and minimal disruption to their existing workflows. The tone is professional and direct, with no jargon. The length is around 800 words. I am going to share three things with you. First, who I am and how I work. Second, who the audience is and what they care about. Third, what success looks like for this proposal. After that, I will ask you to draft.

The AI returns something I can use, usually with edits that take fifteen minutes instead of an hour. The difference is not the AI getting smarter between versions. The AI is the same. The difference is the briefing.

Try this now The sixty-second test
1 minute

Open ChatGPT or Claude in a new tab. Pick any task you do regularly, an email, an outline, a summary. Type a one-line ask. Read what comes back. Now close that tab, open a new one, and take ninety seconds to write three sentences of context before you make the same ask. The gap between the two outputs is the gap this book closes.

If this feels like more work than the first version, you are right. It is more work in the first interaction. The reason it compounds: I save the brief. The next time I need a proposal, I open the file, update three lines, and send it. By the third time, the brief writes itself in under a minute, and the output is consistently better than what I would have produced on my own.

· · ·

If you cannot describe the job in one paragraph, you are not ready to delegate it.

· · ·

The reason this works is mechanical. AI models generate the most likely next words based on what came before them. If what came before was "write me a project proposal," the output is the most likely project proposal in general, which by definition is generic. If what came before was three paragraphs of context about you, your audience, your priorities, and your voice, the output is the most likely proposal given all of that.

§
The plain math

Specific input creates specific output. Generic input creates generic output. There is no other secret here.

Insights and ideas

Five observations to hold while the frame settles in. Each one will come back later in the book, but the moment to plant them is now.

i.
The hire frame works at the level of instinct.

You would never hand a new colleague a one-line ask and expect a good result on day one. You would brief them. The same instinct, applied to AI, produces dramatically better output for the same effort.

ii.
Search-box mode feels faster, but the math is the opposite.

Typing a one-line prompt and getting an instant response feels productive. Five minutes of briefing saves an hour of editing. Compounded across a week, that is most of a workday returned to you.

iii.
The brief is reusable in a way human briefings are not.

A new hire forgets what you told them last week. The brief sits in a file. You open it, paste it, and AI starts already knowing what a colleague would have taken three weeks to absorb. A strong brief can keep working for months at a time, with periodic updates as the work shifts.

iv.
"I do not have time to write briefs" is the wrong objection.

The brief saves you the hour you would have spent editing generic output. The brief is the work that the work makes possible. Skipping it is the most expensive shortcut in this whole book.

v.
The hire frame changes what you do with the output.

When AI gives you back something flat, your move is the same one you would make with a junior colleague: send it back with feedback. Tell it what worked and what did not. Ask for one specific adjustment. This is the editing loop, the topic of Chapter 8.

To do for readers

Total time · 20 minutes Pick one task you do regularly, and run the full briefing experiment from start to finish. Choose something that recurs at least once a month: a status update for your boss, a weekly email to a client, a meeting agenda you keep rewriting. The first time you do this is the slowest. The third time is fast.
  1. i
    Name the task 2 minutes

    Open a blank document. At the top, write one sentence describing what you want to produce, and who it is for. "A weekly status email to my director, summarizing what my team shipped." Keep it short. The sharper this sentence, the easier everything else gets.

  2. ii
    Write the brief 8 minutes

    Write three short paragraphs underneath. Who the work is for (their role, what they care about). What good looks like (length, tone, structure). What to avoid (jargon, fluff, anything you have seen go wrong before). Treat this as a memo to a new hire who will do the work.

  3. iii
    Run the experiment 5 minutes

    Open ChatGPT or Claude. In the first message, paste your one-sentence ask alone, and read what comes back. Open a second chat. Paste the full brief, then ask for the same output. Compare the two responses side by side.

  4. iv
    Save the brief 3 minutes

    Name the document after the task and save it somewhere you will find it again. "Status email to director." "Vendor follow-up." "Monthly board update." This file is the first piece of your artificial brain. By Chapter 11 you will have a small library of them, and you will stop starting from zero.

  5. v
    Reuse it once 2 minutes, next time the task comes up

    The next time the task comes around, open the file, update the three or four lines that change week to week, and paste it into AI. The first time the brief saves you is the moment the frame stops being an idea and becomes a habit.

Checkpoint

If the briefed output was noticeably better than the one-line version, you have everything you need from this chapter. The rest of the book is the same idea, applied at higher and higher leverage. If the difference was small, that is a Chapter 8 problem, and the editing loop will close it for you.

Chapter Two

The Search
Box Mistake

Why most people stay stuck even after they understand the frame.

Read time7 minutes
Practice time30 minutes over one week
DifficultyBeginner
What you need before you start
The ChatGPT or Claude account from Chapter 1
A sticky note and somewhere visible to put it
One week of light self-observation

Description

Reading Chapter 1 and agreeing with it is easy. Keeping the frame is the part that breaks down. I have watched this with hundreds of people who could quote the chapter back to me and still typed a one-line prompt the next morning. The reason is muscle memory.

You have spent twenty years training yourself to interact with software a specific way. You type short queries, scan the results, and move on. That training does not unlearn itself because you read a chapter. The search box lives in your fingers. That is where habits live. This chapter is about the trap, why it is so sticky, and the three small habits that get you out of it.

The real shift is from retrieval to delegation. Google answers questions. AI does work. Those need different inputs. A retrieval tool wants short, specific queries. A delegate wants context, a brief, and judgment. When you type four words into AI, you are using a delegate like a retrieval tool, and the output mirrors the mismatch.

How I do this

I want you to notice three moments in your own behavior over the next week, and then I want you to insert one small habit between them. The whole protocol takes thirty seconds in real time, and a couple of days of attention to install.

Move 01 Catch the instinct

The instinct shows up at the keyboard. Your fingers hover, you feel slightly stuck, and you start typing something like "write me a follow-up email" or "what are the best practices for X" or "summarize this article in three bullets." The prompt is short, the prompt feels efficient, and the prompt is on its way before you have thought about what you actually want.

That moment is the trap. The speed itself is what makes it so costly, because what feels like productivity is editing time you have not yet paid for.

¶
Why this is so hard

Twenty years of Google training lives in your hands. The keyboard remembers, even when the chapter you read last week says otherwise.

Move 02 The pause

Wait thirty seconds before you type a word. That is the whole technique. The first time you try it, the pause feels physically uncomfortable, because your hands want to move and your brain has not given them anything to do. Sit with the discomfort. The pause is the work, even though it does not feel like it.

In those thirty seconds, three questions land. Who is this for, what does good look like, and what context already exists in my head that the AI cannot see. The questions are short, the answers are short, and the pause stays short, but the output that follows is dramatically different.

§
Why the pause feels slow

It makes invisible work visible. The work was always there. You just used to skip it.

Move 03 The reframe phrase

Pick one phrase you can say to yourself when you catch the instinct. The phrase interrupts the search-box habit and routes you back into delegation mode. The one I use is this:

My reframe phrase If I were briefing a new hire on this, what would I tell them before they started?

The phrase does two things. It moves you from "ask" to "brief," and it shifts your sense of the AI from a search engine to a colleague. By the time you finish the sentence in your head, your fingers know what to type.

Try this now The thirty-second pause
2 minutes

The next time you reach for AI today, sit on your hands. Count to thirty. Notice the prompt you almost typed. Then write three sentences of context before you ask for anything. Time how long the editing takes afterward, and compare it to your usual edit time.

· · ·

The pause between the question and the typing is where AI either works for you or wastes your time.

Insights and ideas

Five things to hold in mind while the new habit installs itself.

i.
Google trained you to be brief, and AI requires the opposite.

The frustration is one habit colliding with the new one. The frustration goes away as soon as the new habit takes over, usually within a week of consistent practice.

ii.
The pause is the single most underrated skill in working with AI.

It costs thirty seconds and changes the entire output, and most people skip it because it feels unproductive. That feeling is the trap. The pause is the productivity.

iii.
Productivity and speed look identical from the outside.

A fast generic output is slower than a slow specific output, once you count the editing time. The cheapest way to look productive is to skip the briefing. The cheapest way to actually be productive is to write it.

iv.
Your role shifted from asking to commissioning.

You are a person who assigns work to a junior colleague now, where you used to be a person querying a database. Everything in this book is downstream of that shift.

v.
Every pause weakens the old habit and grows the new one.

After two weeks of catching the instinct, the search-box reflex fades, and the brief frame takes its place. The window between you and a clean habit is shorter than you think.

To do for readers

Total time · 30 minutes across one week This chapter is a habit chapter. The work is spread thin across several days, and the goal is awareness first, then a small new routine. Do not try to do all five steps in one sitting.
  1. i
    Watch yourself for one day Day 1 · observation only

    Spend a single day noticing yourself open AI. Do not change anything. Notice when you reached for it, what you typed, and how short the first prompt was. Awareness comes first. Correction comes later.

  2. ii
    Write your reframe phrase 5 minutes

    Pick a phrase that works for you. Mine is in Move 03 above. Yours can be different. "What would I tell a junior person?" works. "What context does this prompt need?" works. Write it on a sticky note and put it where you will see it when you open your laptop.

  3. iii
    Set the pause rule No time, just intention

    Commit to thirty seconds of pause before any AI prompt for one week. You will forget the first three times. The fourth time you remember, the rule starts to stick. Do not punish yourself for the misses. Notice the catches.

  4. iv
    Rewrite three real prompts 15 minutes

    Pull up three actual conversations you had with AI in the past few days. Look at the first message you sent. Rewrite each one as if you were briefing a colleague who had never seen the task before. Run the new version. Compare what comes back.

  5. v
    Track the ratio 5 minutes at week's end

    At the end of the week, count two numbers. How many prompts you wrote without the pause, and how many with. The ratio tells you where you are. The goal is the ratio moving in the right direction, and perfection is a distraction at this stage.

Checkpoint

If by the end of the week you have caught yourself in search-box mode at least once and corrected it, the habit has started. If the catches are still rare, look at where your hands are when you open AI. The instinct lives in the fingers. The fix lives in the pause.

Chapter Three

The 90/10
Formula

The reason most of the work stays yours, and why that is the point.

Read time8 minutes
Practice time20 minutes
DifficultyBeginner
What you need before you start
A real task you have to do this week
A blank document for the thinking spine
Twenty quiet minutes

Description

There is a number worth holding in your head as you work with AI. Ninety percent of the thinking is yours, ten percent comes from AI. On execution, the ratio flips closer to fifty-fifty, sometimes further. The split varies by task, though the principle does not. AI is the amplifier, and you are the source.

This matters because most people use AI backwards. They try to get AI to do their thinking, and they end up spending their energy editing thoughts that were never theirs. The output reads like generic LinkedIn writing because it is the average of generic LinkedIn writing. The reader cannot find your voice in it because your voice was never in the prompt.

The 90/10 formula is the antidote. It tells you what to bring before you open the tool, and what to ask AI to do once you are in it. The chapters that follow are all variations on this one idea: get the ratio right and AI compounds your work, get it wrong and AI flattens it.

How I do this

Let me walk through how I apply the formula on a real task. Pretend I have to write a short post for my professional network about a topic I care about. The task is small enough to demonstrate, and the moves are identical for tasks ten times bigger.

Move 01 Do the thinking on paper first

Before I open AI for any meaningful task, I write three things by hand or in a blank doc: the point of view I want to take, the audience I am writing for, and the handful of things I know that they do not. I cap this at five minutes. The output is not polished prose. It is a half-formed map of where I want to land.

This is the 90 percent that has to come from you. AI cannot guess what you think about something it has never seen you write about. The reason AI sounds generic when you skip this step is that AI is filling the space your thinking should have filled.

¶
Why this step gets skipped

Writing by hand feels old-fashioned, like preparing for a meeting before showing up. That is exactly what it is, and that is why it works.

Move 02 Feed the thinking into the prompt

Once the map exists, I open AI and paste the thinking into the prompt. I include the parts that matter for this task and leave the rest in the planning doc. The point of view goes in first, the audience goes in second, the observations that anchor my argument go in third, and then the ask comes last.

Prompt: the 90/10 version I want to write a 300-word post for my professional network. My point of view: the people who get the most out of AI today are the ones who treat it like a colleague. Audience: mid-career professionals who are using AI casually but not yet getting much out of it. Three things I know that they probably do not. First, AI works better when you tell it what you already know about the topic before you ask. Second, the time you save with AI shows up in week three, after the briefs start to compound. Third, the people who get the most leverage are the ones who write small briefs and reuse them. Now draft the post in my voice, conversational and direct, with one example from a real workday.

The prompt looks long. The thinking was already done before the prompt existed. The writing part of the prompt took two minutes because I was just describing what I already knew. Give AI the spine, and the manuscript is what you build together afterward.

Try this now Audit one recent output
5 minutes

Open the last thing you wrote with AI's help. Read it slowly. Mark every sentence that sounds like it could have been written by anyone. Those are the sentences where the prompt skipped the thinking step. The marked-up draft is your evidence that the 90 percent matters.

Move 03 Let AI do the execution work

Once AI starts producing, my job shifts. I read what comes back and I check it against the spine I gave it. The parts that match the spine, I keep. The parts that drift, I push back on. The parts where AI adds something I had not thought of, I evaluate honestly. Sometimes AI surprises me with a sharper formulation of something I half-saw, and that is the 10 percent of thinking AI can contribute, worth keeping when it shows up.

Move 01 Pick a task you actually need

Fake tasks produce fake feedback. You cannot tell if the output is good because you have no real criterion. Real tasks come with a real bar, and the bar is what teaches you what the tool can do.

Prompts that work follow this shape, even when they look different on the surface.

The first prompt is rarely the best one.

iv.
Reading the output slowly is the most underrated step.

Beginners scan the output for the parts they like and ignore the rest. The parts you ignore are the diagnostic information. Slow reading turns the output into a map of what your prompt missed.

v.
The shape of a working prompt transfers across tasks.

Context, task, format. The shape works for emails, summaries, plans, analyses, and almost anything else. Once you internalize the shape, every new task starts ten minutes ahead of where it used to.

How I do this

Let me walk you through the editing loop in action. I will start with a typical bad output, walk through how I read it, and show what I change in the prompt to get the second attempt right. The whole loop takes a few minutes and replaces a long, frustrating back-and-forth.

Move 01 Read the output to find the prompt's gap

When AI returns something flat, the answer to "what is wrong" is almost always in the prompt. Read what came back and ask one question: what would I have had to tell the AI for this output to come out closer to right. The answer points you at the gap in the prompt. The gap is what you fix.

¶
The retry trap

Each follow-up message carries the previous bad output forward in the conversation. The model uses it as reference for the next attempt. The bad context corrupts the next answer. Editing the prompt is how you escape it.

Move 02 Edit, do not reply

Hover over your original prompt. Find the edit button, a pencil icon in ChatGPT, an edit option in Claude. Rewrite the prompt with the gap filled. Send it. The model starts fresh from the corrected prompt, with none of the drift from your previous follow-ups. Three rounds of patching collapse into one clean rewrite.

Example — what the edit looks like Original prompt: "Draft a follow-up email to the client." Bad output: too long, too apologetic. Edited prompt: "Draft a follow-up email to the client. Under 100 words. No apologetic language. Open with a question. End with a specific next step." Same chat, same starting point, different output.

The first edit will get you closer. That is normal. Read the new output, find the new gap, edit again. Two or three iterations usually land you somewhere useful. Stop iterating when you have an output you can finish by hand in five minutes, because that is the point where further prompting costs more than just doing it yourself.

Description

Many prompts fail at the first sentence. The reason is small, and once you see it you cannot unsee it: the prompt is shaped like a question when the task is shaped like a job. Questions invite information. Jobs invite work. AI happily produces either, and the output is dramatically different depending on which shape the prompt arrived in.

The shift is easy to miss because it looks like a writing tweak. "What are the best practices for onboarding new hires?" arrives at AI as a question, and the model returns a generic list. "Draft a one-page onboarding checklist for a new senior product manager joining a B2B software company" arrives as a job, and the model returns a draft you can use. The topic is identical. The output is unrecognizable between the two.

This chapter teaches the shift from question to job, with worked examples and the rule of thumb that catches almost any misuse before you hit send.

How I do this

Three moves to shift any question prompt into a job prompt. I run this mental check on almost every prompt I write, and the check takes about three seconds once the habit is installed.

Move 01 Notice the question shape

Question prompts open with words like What, How, Why, Should, or When. They ask AI to retrieve information or generate options. Question shape is useful when information is what you actually want. The trouble starts when the goal is work and the prompt arrives shaped like a question by default. The model returns the information you asked for, and you spend the next thirty minutes turning that information into the work you actually needed.

Move 02 Rewrite as an imperative

Take the question prompt and rewrite it as an imperative. "What are the best practices for onboarding?" becomes "Draft an onboarding checklist for a new senior product manager." "How do I structure a brand strategy?" becomes "Write the opening structure of a brand strategy for a fintech company entering Europe." "Should we change vendors?" becomes "Build the case for changing vendors, with three reasons in order of strength." The imperative names the deliverable, and the deliverable is what the question shape leaves out.

¶
Real questions still belong

Questions earn their place when information is what you actually need. "What are the common failure modes of asynchronous onboarding?" is a real question, and the answer is useful. The trap is using question shape when work is the goal.

Move 03 Add the deliverable shape

A job prompt benefits from naming what the finished work looks like. A draft, a checklist, a memo, a list of options, a decision. State the deliverable explicitly inside the prompt. AI will still produce something if you skip this step, and the shape will be the model's default guess instead of your actual need.

Description

AI without constraints will give you something useful in the shape AI thinks is best. AI with constraints will give you something useful in the shape you actually want. The difference between the two is one or two short sentences at the bottom of your prompt. The trade is asymmetric: three seconds of constraint saves three minutes of edit.

Constraints come in three flavors: format (memo, email, bullets, tables), length (200 words, three paragraphs, one page), and tone (formal, conversational, urgent, warm). Each one shapes the output independently, and together they shape it completely.

This chapter teaches the three constraint moves and the rule of thumb for when to use which. By the end you will have a small library of constraint patterns that work across most of your recurring tasks.

How I do this

Three moves, one per constraint type. The whole thing fits in one sentence at the end of any prompt. Constraints are the cheapest sharpening tool in the prompt-writing kit.

Move 01 Format

Tell AI what shape the output should take. The options include a memo, a list, a table, a draft email, a two-column comparison, or a structured outline. The format sets the structure before any content lands. AI struggles when it has to guess the format, and it picks safe defaults that rarely match what you wanted.

Move 02 Length

Tell AI how long the output should be. Word count, sentence count, paragraph count, or a comparative like "under one screen." AI does not have a strong native sense of length, and without a constraint it tends to run long. With a constraint, it lands close to the target almost every time.

Move 03 Tone

Tell AI how the output should feel. The vocabulary that works includes single words like conversational, direct, warm, formal, urgent, or skeptical, and short phrases like "warm and direct, with zero buzzwords." The tone constraint catches the part of the output that format and length leave open.

¶
Combining constraints

All three constraints can fit in one sentence. "Under 200 words, in a memo format, warm and direct, with zero hedging." The sentence covers format, length, and tone, and it adds a meta-constraint about avoiding hedging language. The whole sentence becomes a template you reuse across hundreds of prompts.

How I do this

Three moves to apply the hire-test well. The first is the job description test. The second is the reliability test. The third is the retirement decision.

Move 01 The job description test

If the agent were a person, what is their job description in one sentence. The agent should have a job description that you could explain to a colleague in under thirty seconds. Over the last ten runs, how often did the agent produce usable output on the first try. If the answer is eight or more, the agent is reliable. If the answer is five to seven, the agent needs iteration before it earns its place. For agents that fail both tests, the retirement decision is honest. Retire them. The work moves back to chat or back to your hands. The retirement is not a failure of the agent or of you. It is a recognition that the work did not benefit from being packaged at this scope, and the time you would have spent maintaining the agent goes back into your real work.

How I do this

Three moves to take the practice forward after the last page. The first is staying current. The second is practicing with new tools. The third is teaching what you have learned.

Move 01 Stay current with the right sources

The field generates more news than any reader can follow. The right move is to follow a small number of sources that filter for you. Pick three sources: a newsletter that summarizes the week, a podcast that goes deep on one topic, a blog or YouTube channel that demonstrates new tools as they arrive. Three is the right number, since fewer is too narrow and more becomes noise. Set a weekly slot to read or listen, with the rest of the week left for actual practice.

Move 02 Practice with new tools as they arrive

New tools arrive every quarter. Some are major releases. Some are small. The discipline is to test the major releases with one real task before deciding whether to adopt. Set up a thirty-minute slot every quarter for new tool exploration. Use one real task as the benchmark. If the new tool produces better output on the benchmark, switch. If the output matches your current setup, stay. The test saves you from chasing every release and keeps you from missing the ones that matter.

Move 03 Teach what you have learned

The most important move after closing the book is teaching what you learned. Write a blog post about your AI practice. Explain the About-Me file to a colleague over lunch. Show your weekly pulse to your team. Each act of teaching forces you to make explicit what has become automatic, and the act of articulation is itself a sharpening exercise.
